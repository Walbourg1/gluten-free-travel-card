Gluten-free travel: 12 countries and how to eat safely

What to expect, what to avoid, and the words that matter

Eating gluten-free abroad is less about the country's cuisine than about how well the kitchen understands the request. Some places make it almost effortless; others hide wheat in things that look entirely safe. Here is what to expect in twelve common destinations.

Before you go

Two habits make the biggest difference. Learn the local phrase for gluten-free — it is in the list below — and carry something you can show, because explaining cross-contamination in a second language under time pressure rarely works. A printed card or an offline app both do the job; the point is that it does not depend on the restaurant's wifi.

Country by country

Italy

On the ground: Probably the easiest country in Europe. Awareness is high, "senza glutine" is understood everywhere, and pharmacies stock gluten-free food. Watch out for fresh pasta and anything breaded.

Spain

On the ground: "Sin gluten" is widely understood and labelling is decent. The risk sits in tapas: croquetas, flour-thickened sauces, and shared fryers.

„Gluten-free diet" → DIETA SIN GLUTEN · View the card

France

On the ground: Awareness is improving, but bread is everywhere and sauces are traditionally thickened with flour. Buckwheat galettes sound safe but often share a griddle with wheat crêpes.

„Gluten-free diet" → RÉGIME SANS GLUTEN · View the card

Greece

On the ground: Grilled meat, fish and salads make it manageable. The traps are phyllo pastry, pita served with everything, and shared frying oil.

Portugal

On the ground: Similar to Spain but with less labelling. Breaded fish is a staple, and many sauces are flour-based.

„Gluten-free diet" → DIETA SEM GLÚTEN · View the card

Turkey

On the ground: Bread accompanies nearly every meal, and bulgur appears in dishes that look safe. Grilled meat is usually your best bet.

„Gluten-free diet" → GLUTENSİZ BESLENME · View the card

Croatia

On the ground: Awareness is growing along the coast. Grilled fish is straightforward; breaded cutlets and thickened stews are not.

„Gluten-free diet" → BEZGLUTENSKA PREHRANA · View the card

Austria

On the ground: German-language labelling is reliable and "glutenfrei" is well understood. The problem is the cuisine itself: Schnitzel, dumplings and Spätzle are all wheat.

„Gluten-free diet" → GLUTENFREIE ERNÄHRUNG · View the card

Netherlands

On the ground: Good labelling and near-universal English make communication easy. The main risk is the shared fryer behind every snack counter.

„Gluten-free diet" → GLUTENVRIJ DIEET · View the card

Poland

On the ground: Awareness is rising and "bezglutenowy" appears on menus in cities. Pierogi, breaded cutlets and barley soups are the classic traps.

„Gluten-free diet" → DIETA BEZGLUTENOWA · View the card

Thailand

On the ground: Rice-based cooking helps enormously, but soy sauce and oyster sauce go into almost everything — including dishes that contain no visible wheat.

„Gluten-free diet" → อาหารปราศจากกลูเตน · View the card

Japan

On the ground: Rice is everywhere, which sounds ideal — but standard soy sauce (shoyu) contains wheat, and so do many miso and dashi preparations. Tempura is out.

„Gluten-free diet" → グルテンフリー食 · View the card

The pattern across all of them

Three risks repeat almost everywhere, regardless of cuisine: flour used as a thickener, shared frying oil, and equipment that touched bread earlier in the service. None of these are visible on the plate, which is why a card that asks for clean pans is worth more than one that only lists foods.
